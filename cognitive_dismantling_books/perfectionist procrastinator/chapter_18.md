# Chapter 18: Energy

There is a myth that the perfectionist is a high-energy person. The image is of a tireless worker, powered by ambition. If you are a perfectionist, you know the truth: you are exhausted. Not occasionally. Constantly. And you cannot understand why, because you are not doing anything.

That last sentence is the key. You are not doing anything. You are spinning. And spinning is the most exhausting activity there is, because it produces no forward motion and consumes all your fuel.

Let me show you the energy ledger of perfectionism.

The normal worker approaches a task. They expend energy on the work itself. When the work is done, the expenditure stops. They rest. The energy returns. Simple.

The perfectionist approaches the same task. They expend energy on the work. Then they expend energy on the fear: the dreading, the re-checking, the self-criticism. Then they expend energy on the polishing. Then they expend energy on the *vigilance*: the background hum of the unfinished project that follows them everywhere. By the end of the day, they have done less work than the normal worker, but they are three times as tired.

This is why perfectionists so often feel like they are running on fumes. They are. They are burning fuel on a treadmill.

Here is the deeper drain, and it is the one nobody talks about: **perfectionism consumes energy *before* the work even begins.** Think about the last time you faced a project you cared about. What did you do first? You did not begin. You prepared. You thought about how hard it would be. You imagined the deadline. You rehearsed the failure. And by the time you actually sat down to work, you were already tired. The perfectionist spends their energy before the work, bracing for it. The bracing is invisible, so the exhaustion looks mysterious. It is not mysterious. It is the cost of meeting every task with the full apparatus of dread.

There is also the exhaustion of decision. Every perfectionist carries a heavy decision load, because they refuse to decide anything until it can be decided perfectly. What to write. How to phrase it. Whether to send it. Each micro-decision is a drain, and the perfectionist makes hundreds of them a day, and re-makes them, because the decisions are never final. The normal worker decides and moves on. The perfectionist decides, revises, re-decides, and moves nowhere. The same decisions, paid for again and again.

And finally, there is the exhaustion of the shame. The perfectionist's internal monologue is a running commentary of failure. "Not good enough." "You should have done better." This commentary is not neutral; it is a drain. Criticism takes energy to absorb, even self-criticism, and the perfectionist absorbs a steady stream of it all day. They are being attacked by their own voice, from morning to night.

Now, here is the energy promise of the other way of working. When you ship a task—not polish it, ship it—you experience a phenomenon the perfectionist almost never feels: the surge. It is the release of the held tension, the closing of the loop, the quiet drop of the shoulders. It is a genuine recharge. The person who ships four tasks a day receives four surges. The person who polishes one task a day receives none—only the slow, grinding expenditure, with no relief at the end.

This is why the ship-and-iterate worker seems to have boundless energy. They do not have more energy than you. They have a working method that *returns* energy instead of consuming it. Every finished task gives a little back. The perfectionist's method gives nothing back. It only takes.

I want to be honest about the first few days of change. When you first stop polishing, you will not feel the surge immediately. The compulsion to polish will pull at you, and resisting it will cost energy. For a few days, you may feel worse, not better.

But then something shifts. The loops close. The hum quiets. And you will begin to notice, with a kind of wonder, that you are not tired all the time anymore. The energy that was being poured into the treadmill is flowing back into you.

You have not been lazy. You have been spending your energy on a treadmill, and the treadmill pays nothing.

**EXERCISE: The Energy Audit**

Put the book down. Take your paper. Write down your last working day, hour by hour. For each hour, mark what it was actually spent on: working, polishing, bracing, worrying, deciding and re-deciding, or resting.

Now, for each hour that was spent bracing, worrying, or re-deciding, write one word: *preventable.*

Look at the page. Most of your day was spent on preventable expenditure. The work itself needs your energy. The fear, the polishing, and the vigilance do not. Cut the preventable column, and the energy returns.

You were never running out. You were being drained by a phantom.
