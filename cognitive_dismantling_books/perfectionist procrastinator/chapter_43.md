# Chapter 43: Help the Perfectionist Left on the Sinking Ship

Now that you have escaped the trap, you will feel a natural urge to help others. You will see friends, family members, or colleagues who are still polishing, still holding their work hostage to the flawless standard, still drowning in the drafts you just left behind.

You will see the panic in their eyes when the deadline arrives and nothing is ready. You will hear the familiar excuses: "It's not quite there yet," or "I want to get it right."

You know the truth: **it will never be "quite there." Not until they release it.**

Your instinct will be to shake them. You'll want to say, "Just ship it! It's good enough!"

**Don't do this.**

Remember how you felt when people told you to "just relax your standards." Did it help? Or did it just make you feel misunderstood, judged, and more certain that they didn't grasp how much the work mattered to you?

Perfectionists are already drowning in self-judgment. They feel like the only ones who care. If you approach them with logic or impatience, they will hear criticism. And criticism is a trigger for the Perfectionist type. It makes them double down on the very standard that is imprisoning them.

So, how do you help?

**1. Don't become the enabler.**

In the past, you might have colluded with them. You might have said, "Yeah, take your time," or "It's worth waiting for the best version." You were validating their addiction to the sealed work.

Stop doing that. You don't have to be mean, but don't feed the Monster. If they say, "I'm not ready to send it yet," don't say, "That's fine, whenever you're ready." Say nothing. Or say, gently: "It's ready enough. The world is waiting for the real version, not the perfect one."

**2. Model the release.**

The most powerful argument is your own behavior.

Let them see you shipping. Let them see you releasing imperfect work without apology, without the preemptive self-criticism, without the six-hour polish. "Here's the draft. It's not perfect, but it's done." Don't brag. Just do it, visibly, calmly, repeatedly.

They will notice. They will wonder why you seem so unbothered. They will wonder why your work keeps getting out into the world while theirs stays sealed on the desk. Eventually, they will ask: *"How do you do it?"*

**3. Use the key question.**

When they are in the middle of a panic about the work "not being good enough," ask them gently:

*"What would actually happen if you released it as it is right now?"*

Don't wait for an answer. Just plant the seed. Let them realize that the catastrophe they are protecting against is a fiction, and the cost of the protection—the unreleased work, the anxiety, the years—is real.

**4. If they ask for help...**

If they genuinely ask for help, don't give them a system. Don't buy them an app. Don't teach them better revision techniques.

Give them the key. Tell them about the flawless standard, and that it was never real. Tell them about the relief of the sealed work, and that the relief is the trap. Tell them about the release, and that it does not kill.

Tell them: *"You aren't too fussy. You aren't too demanding. You are just afraid that the verdict will hurt. It will not hurt as much as the holding. The holding is the hurt."*

Be patient. You know how strong the belief can be. But you also know the way out. You walked it.

The best way to help the perfectionist on the sinking ship is to stand firmly on the shore, releasing your own imperfect work in full view, and let them watch what freedom looks like.

They will not believe you at first. The belief is too old, too dear to them. But the evidence will work on them, the way it worked on you: not through argument, but through demonstration.

And one day, they will release something. Imperfect. Real. And they will survive. And they will come to you, and they will not need to say anything, because you will know.

That is the moment you will understand why you wrote this chapter.

**EXERCISE: The Example**

Put the book down.

Think of the perfectionist you know who is most trapped. The colleague, the friend, the family member whose work never leaves their desk.

Now, without saying a word to them about this book, show them the freedom. Release your own next piece of work in their line of sight, imperfect and unashamed. Let them see the relief on your face when it goes out.

You cannot argue them out of the trap. The trap does not respond to argument. It responds to evidence.

You are the evidence.
