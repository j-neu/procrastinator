# Chapter 30: Will I Become a Mediocre Hack?

There is a fear at the bottom of the perfectionist's heart, deeper than the fear of judgment. It is the fear that the perfectionism itself is the only thing holding the quality together. And it has a name you will recognize the moment I say it:

*"If I stop being a perfectionist, I'll become a mediocre hack."*

You have probably never said this out loud. But it is there, at the bottom, and it is the reason every argument in this book has had to fight so hard. What if the standard is not the trap? What if it is the only thing between you and sloppiness?

Let me take this fear apart, slowly, because it is the strongest one you have, and it deserves the most careful treatment.

First, notice what the fear assumes. It assumes there are two versions of you: the perfectionist, who produces excellent work, and the relaxed version, who would produce mediocre work. And it assumes the only difference between them is the standard.

Both assumptions are false.

The excellent work you have produced was not produced by the standard. It was produced by your skill, your experience, your judgment, your accumulated practice. The standard was there, adding anxiety, not quality. Strip the standard away and the skill remains. You do not lose your eye for good work when you stop being a perfectionist. The eye is not the standard; the eye is you.

Here is the second thing. The fear assumes that "mediocre hack" is a destination you can fall into. It is not. There is no such place. The phrase is a bogeyman, invented by the fear to keep you gripping the standard. When you ship imperfect work, you do not become a mediocre hack. You become a person who ships imperfect work—which is to say, a person who ships. Every human being who has ever finished anything is a person who ships imperfect work.

Let me show you the flaw in the fear by taking it to its logical end. If the standard is the thing that produces quality, then the person with the highest standards should produce the most quality. But the person with the highest standards is the person who ships the least. The perfectionist, the most demanding person in the room, produces the smallest body of work. If the standard were the engine of quality, this would be impossible. The standard does not produce quality. It produces paralysis.

Now here is the question you must ask yourself, because it is the question that undoes the entire fear: **what would a mediocre hack do?**

A mediocre hack would not care. A mediocre hack would release work without thought, without revision, without shame. A mediocre hack would be satisfied with the first draft. A mediocre hack would not lie awake at night worrying about the flawed paragraph.

Do you recognize yourself in any of that? No. Because you are not a mediocre hack, and you cannot become one. The person who worries about becoming mediocre is, by definition, not mediocre. The mediocrity you fear is not a thing you could fall into. It is a thing you have been using as a threat to keep yourself in the trap.

Here is the deeper truth, and I want you to feel it in your bones. Your fear is backwards. It is not the standard that keeps you excellent. It is the shipping that keeps you excellent. The person who ships, learns, and ships again is constantly improving. The person who polishes the same work forever is standing still. You have been protecting a standard that was holding you in place, while the "mediocre hacks" you despise have been shipping, learning, and quietly getting better than you. The relaxed worker is not your fall; the relaxed worker is your future.

There is one more thing, and it is the thing I most want you to remember from this chapter. You are afraid that loosening your grip will make you ordinary. But you are already ordinary. Not in the sense of being unremarkable—in the sense of being human. Your work is human work. It will have human flaws. It will be judged by human judges. And the only way to be anything other than a person with a sealed, useless idea is to accept that humanity and release the work.

The choice was never between excellent and mediocre. The choice is between releasing and hiding. The excellent work of your life is on the other side of the releases. Every release is a rep, and every rep makes the next work better. The standard has been holding the work hostage. Let it go, and the work—real, flawed, improving—finally gets out into the world.

You will not become a mediocre hack. You will become something the fear has never let you see: a person who finishes.

**EXERCISE: The Bogeyman Test**

Put the book down. Take your paper. Write down the sentence you fear most: *"If I stop being a perfectionist, I will become mediocre."*

Now write the evidence for it. Not the feeling. The evidence. When in your life did relaxing your standards—even slightly—produce worse work? Really search for it.

You will find the evidence is thin, or absent. The "mediocrity" you fear is a prediction, not a memory. It has never happened.

Finally, write the sentence that replaces it: *"The standard never produced my quality. My skill did. My skill is still here. The standard was only ever the fear, and the fear is what I am releasing."*

Then ship the next thing. The bogeyman will not be waiting on the other side. Only the work will.
