# Chapter 13: "Combination" Tasks

Some tasks tempt the perfectionist. Others terrify them. And a special few do both at once. These are combination tasks, and they are the ones that bring the perfectionist to a complete standstill.

A combination task has two ingredients. The first is high stakes: the result will be seen, judged, and remembered. The second is a quality-sensitive process: the result must be *good*, not merely done. When these two ingredients meet, the perfectionist's fear engine switches on at full power.

Consider the examples. The report to the board. The application that decides the next stage of your career. The creative piece that will be read by strangers. In each case, the task is not just "do this"; it is "do this, and be judged on how well you do it."

Now ask yourself: when was the last time you polished a combination task to completion? If you are a perfectionist, the honest answer is probably: rarely, or never. These are the tasks that live in your folder of great intentions.

Here is what makes the combination task the perfectionist's perfect trap. It is the one task where the fear of judgment is *partially justified*. The report will be judged. The stakes are real, and the quality matters. This is the excuse the perfectionist needs: "This task deserves my standards." So the standards go up, and up, and the paralysis becomes total.

But notice what actually happens. The perfectionist does not respond to the combination task by producing excellent work. They respond by producing *no* work. The board receives no report. The application is never submitted. The task that was worth doing well ends up not being done at all.

Let me put this plainly: **the combination task punishes perfectionism more than any other kind of task.** The stakes mean the work must be released into judgment. The quality means the work must be good enough to survive. But the perfectionist's response—refuse to release it—means the work is never judged and never good, because it does not exist.

The irony is total. The perfectionist, who cares more than anyone about quality, is the only person who cannot complete the tasks where quality matters most.

How does the non-perfectionist handle a combination task? They do not lower the stakes; the stakes are what they are. But they apply a different logic. They say: "This has to be good, so I will start now, make it as good as I can, and ship it on the deadline." The deadline is the ship date. Quality is pursued *within* the ship date, not instead of it.

The perfectionist reverses this logic. They say: "This has to be good, so I will not ship it until it is good enough." The ship date disappears. The quality bar rises to infinity. The task is never done.

Here is the thing the perfectionist must accept to be free: **there is no such thing as a quality task without a deadline, because without a deadline there is no finished work, and without finished work there is no quality.** The unwritten report has the quality of a void. The perfect, never-shipped proposal is indistinguishable from no proposal at all. Quality is not a property of your intention; it is a property of the thing that exists. And the thing only exists because someone, at some point, let it go.

I am not asking you to be careless with the important work. I am asking you to see that your current approach is the most careless possible approach: it guarantees the work never exists at all. A rough report that ships is worth more than a perfect report that never leaves your desk.

There is a quieter version of this trap, and it catches the perfectionist on the small tasks too. It is not only the board report that triggers the combination effect. It is any task where you can imagine a version of yourself doing it *beautifully*. The email you want to be perfectly phrased. Every one of these has stakes and quality, and every one can trap you for an afternoon.

The rule is the same at every scale: the perfectionist has confused "it should be good" with "it must be perfect." Good is a destination. Perfect is a ghost. The combination task is not asking you for a ghost. It is asking you for good, on a deadline, out in the world.

**EXERCISE: The Combination Task**

Put the book down. Take your paper. Write down the combination task you are currently avoiding. The one with real stakes. Now write the deadline that would exist if you took the task seriously. Write today's date. Then write the date you will ship it.

Now write one line below it, and mean it: *"The stakes are real, so the work will be released. I will make it as good as I can by the ship date. Nothing more. Nothing less."*

The perfectionist's version of this task has never been finished. The shipped version starts now.
