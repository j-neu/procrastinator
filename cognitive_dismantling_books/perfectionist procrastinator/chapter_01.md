# Chapter 1: The Most Demanding Person I Have Yet to Meet

Perhaps I should begin by describing my competence for writing this book. No, I am not a psychologist or a productivity guru; my qualifications are far more appropriate. I spent twenty-five years of my life as a confirmed perfectionist. In the later years, I was shipping late, revising endlessly, and living in a state of permanent dissatisfaction with everything I produced.

I made dozens of attempts to "raise my standards." I once spent three weeks planning the perfect novel, the notebooks, the outlines, the system. Then I stared at the blank first page for a month, certain that anything I wrote would be unworthy of the vision in my head. The novel was never written. The notebooks are still beautiful.

With most perfectionists, it's a question of "I'll do it when I can do it justice." I had reached the stage where I knew that moment would never arrive. I had a permanent knot in my stomach from the knowledge that a dozen projects sat unfinished because they couldn't be flawless. I honestly believed that any moment my reputation would collapse, and I would be exposed as the fraud I felt I was. It bothered me, but it still didn't stop me from polishing the same paragraph for the fourth hour.

I had reached the stage where I gave up even trying to finish things. Most perfectionists suffer from the illusion that their high standards are their greatest asset. But I never suffered from that illusion. I have always detested the feeling of treading water, but I thought the standards were protecting me. I was terrified of what would happen if I ever relaxed them.

Eventually, my editor sent me to a writing retreat. I was completely skeptical, having visions of a guru chanting about "letting go." The instructor tried to make me write "badly" on purpose. Nothing appeared to be working. I didn't suddenly love rough drafts, or at least I didn't think I did, and yet after that realization (not the retreat itself, but the *realization*), not only did I start finishing things, but I actually enjoyed the process of shipping imperfect work.

Now, before you go rushing off to a retreat, let me make something quite clear. Writing tricks are just tools. If the mindset is wrong, you won't stop polishing. I would be buried under a mountain of unfinished projects by now if I hadn't found a way out. But it was in spite of the tools, not because of them.

During those awful years, I thought that my value depended on never shipping anything less than excellent. I was prepared to lose opportunities, money, and peace of mind rather than release work that had a single flaw. Today people ask me whether I ever feel the urge to redo, revise, and restart. The answer is, "Never, never, never," just the reverse. I've had a marvelous life since I woke up. The most marvelous thing that has ever happened to me is being freed from that slavery of trying to make everything perfect while the world quietly moved on without me.

Let me make it quite clear: I am not a "naturally relaxed" person. I have a demanding brain. I started reading up on psychology and creativity. Nothing I read explained the miracle that had happened. Why had it been so ridiculously easy to stop polishing, whereas previously it had been years of grey fog? I was going about it back to front. I was trying to work out why it had been so easy to start shipping, whereas the real problem is explaining why perfectionists find it so difficult. The weight didn't exist in the work itself. It was all in the mind.

My full-time profession is now helping other perfectionists to get free. Let me emphasize from the start: there is no such thing as a "hopeless case." Anybody can not only stop polishing but also find it easy. It is basically fear that keeps us polishing: the fear that without the standards we will become mediocre, and the fear of being judged on a result that isn't flawless. Nothing could be further from the truth.

All perfectionists can find it easy to stop, even you! All you have to do is read the rest of the book with an open mind. Let me issue a warning. There are only two reasons for failure with my method:

1.  **Failure to Carry Out Instructions.** I am dogmatic about certain recommendations, and the reason is that I know my subject. Everything I tell you has a purpose: to make it easy to stop.
2.  **Failure to Understand.** Question not only what I tell you but also your own views and what society has taught you about "high standards." Ask yourselves why a habit that makes you miserable and ruins your peace is so difficult to break.

**EXERCISE: The Cost of the Standard**

Put this book down. Find a separate piece of paper or open a note on your phone. List three things you have "polished" out of existence in the last year, projects you never shipped because they weren't good enough yet. Next to each, write what you were protecting yourself from. Now write how you feel about it right now. Does the protection feel worth it?

This is the "perfect trap." We are going to dismantle it.
