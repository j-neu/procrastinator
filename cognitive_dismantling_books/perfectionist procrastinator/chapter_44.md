# Chapter 44: Advice to Non-Perfectionists

This chapter is written for the people around you. The colleagues, the friends, the partners, the parents. They are not reading this book. You are. So I am writing this chapter for you to give them, or for you to use, because the people around you are part of the trap, whether they know it or not.

The non-perfectionist does not understand the trap. This is not their fault. They look at the perfectionist and see a hard worker who is too hard on themselves. And because they do not understand the trap, they say the wrong things, and the wrong things make it worse.

Here is the first piece of advice for the non-perfectionist: **do not say "just do your best."**

"Just do your best" is the most corrosive sentence in the English language, when spoken to a perfectionist. It sounds kind. It is not kind. The perfectionist has never once known what "best" means, because their best is the flawless version, and the flawless version does not exist. "Just do your best" tells the perfectionist to do the impossible, and then leaves them to fail at it alone.

Instead, say the thing that is actually true: "The version you have is enough. Ship it." The perfectionist needs permission, not instruction. They need to be told that the work, as it is, can go out. That the release is the standard. That "done" is a real thing.

Here is the second piece of advice: **do not praise the effort. Praise the release.**

The perfectionist has been praised for their striving their whole life. "You're so dedicated." "You never settle." "You're such a perfectionist." Every one of these praises is a reward for staying trapped.

The praise that helps is the praise of the thing released. "I read your report. The argument in the third section was strong." "I'm glad you sent that. It was useful." The praise must land on the shipped work, not the hidden process. Praise the release, and you teach them the lesson the trap has been hiding all their lives.

Here is the third piece of advice: **do not criticize the process. Never say "you're too much of a perfectionist."**

The label is a verdict, and the verdict confirms the fear. The perfectionist already believes they are the problem. Tell them they are too much of a perfectionist, and you give the shame a new costume.

Instead, speak about the behavior, gently, as a thing that can change. "I noticed you were still working on the report at midnight. The report can be good enough. You can be done." The distinction is everything. "You are a perfectionist" is an identity, and identities are defended. "The report can be done" is a fact, and facts can be acted on.

Here is the fourth piece of advice, and it is the most important: **never take their perfectionism personally.**

This one is for the partners, the friends, the family. The perfectionist will disappoint you. They will be late. They will cancel. They will be present but elsewhere, thinking about the unfinished work. It is not about you. It is the trap, and the trap has had them for years.

Do not say "you don't care about me." They care. That is not the problem. The problem is that they have never learned that the work can be released, and so the work has been eating their life. Your patience is not indulgence. Your patience is the lifeline. The perfectionist is already drowning in self-judgment; the last thing they need is judgment from the person they love.

And here is the fifth piece of advice, the final one: **model the release.**

The perfectionist cannot be argued out of the trap. The trap does not respond to argument. It responds to evidence. So be the evidence. Ship your own imperfect things. Make your own mistakes in full view. Release your work, your drafts, your half-finished thoughts, and let the perfectionist watch you survive.

They need to see it. They have never once seen a human being release imperfect work and survive. You can show them it is not.

If you are the non-perfectionist reading this, I thank you. You are not the cause of the trap, and you are not the cure. The cure is inside the perfectionist, and it is already working, because they have read this book. Your job is simpler and harder than the cure. Your job is to stop feeding the trap, and to stand as evidence that the release is survivable.

Do not say "just do your best." Say "ship it."

Do not praise the effort. Praise the release.

Do not judge the person. Address the behavior.

Do not take it personally. It was never about you.

And model the release, so they can see that the world does not end.

They are almost free. Your grace can be the last door.

**EXERCISE: The Letter to the Non-Perfectionist**

Put the book down. Take your paper. Write a short letter to the person closest to you, in your own words, telling them the two or three sentences you most need to hear. The ones that would have helped you when you were trapped.

"Please don't tell me to do my best. Tell me it's enough to ship."
"Please don't praise my perfectionism. Praise the things I actually finish."
"Please be patient. I'm learning to let go."

Keep the letter. Give it to them, or keep it for yourself. Either way, write it.

The trap kept you silent about what you needed for years. The silence fed it.

The silence is over.
