# Chapter 9: Stress

Ask a perfectionist why they hold such high standards, and at some point they will say this: "If I let my standards slip, everything will fall apart. The stress would be unbearable."

The perfectionist believes the standards are managing their stress. This is the opposite of the truth. The standards are manufacturing it.

Let me show you the arithmetic. There are two kinds of stress in the perfectionist's life. The first is the stress of the work itself, the deadline, the difficulty. This stress is real, but it is finite. It ends when the work ends. Every worker feels it, and every worker survives it.

The second stress is the stress of the standard. This stress has no end. It begins when you demand that the work be flawless, and it continues as long as the work is unfinished. It is a background radiation that follows you into the weekend, into bed, into the moments you were supposed to be enjoying.

This second stress is the one that destroys perfectionists, and it is entirely self-created. The work stress is the price of doing anything difficult. The standard stress is the price of refusing to let the work be finished.

Watch a perfectionist work and you will see the tell. The shoulders are up. The jaw is tight. The eyes scan the page for the next flaw before the current one is fixed. They are not working; they are bracing. They are in the posture of a person awaiting a blow. The blow never comes from outside. It comes from the voice inside that says, every few minutes, "still not good enough."

Now the stress compounds, because the perfectionist carries the unfinished work everywhere. The report that is "not ready" sits in the bag. The perfectionist cannot enjoy a meal, a film, or a holiday without the project calling to them from the corner of the mind. Every hour of supposed relaxation is spent half-attending to the thing that was never released.

Here is the paradox that defines the perfectionist's life: **the standard that was supposed to reduce judgment has produced a lifetime of self-judgment, and the diligence that was supposed to reduce stress has produced a permanent state of low-grade panic.**

And here is the deeper irony. The perfectionist is often praised for being "so hard on themselves," as if self-criticism were a kind of fuel. It is not fuel. It is friction. The self-critical worker is not more productive; they are more exhausted, more anxious, and more likely to abandon projects entirely. Stress does not sharpen judgment. It blunts it. The exhausted perfectionist makes more errors, notices more flaws, polishes more, ships less, and grows more stressed. The loop feeds itself.

I want to be clear about what I am not saying. I am not telling you that work is stress-free. Difficult work is demanding. But there is a profound difference between the stress of effort and the stress of impossibility. The worker who accepts that the report will be imperfect feels the stress of effort: pressure, focus, a healthy urgency. The perfectionist feels the stress of impossibility: the weight of a demand that can never be met. One of these stresses ends. The other never does.

Consider the relief that comes when you finally, reluctantly, ship a project, the exhaustion followed by the lightness. For a day or two, the background radiation switches off. The perfectionist feels, briefly, what normal people feel all the time: that the project is over, that life is not an endless siege.

The question I want to leave you with is simple: why would you choose to live in the siege?

The standards do not keep you safe. They keep you tense. They do not protect your reputation. They hide your work. The stress of the standard is a tax you pay for a protection that was never offered. You can stop paying it at any moment. The work will still be hard. But the impossible demand, the demand that it be flawless, that demand can be resigned from.

You do not have to lower your standards to feel relief. You only have to stop pretending that an impossible demand is the same thing as a high one.

**EXERCISE: The Two Ledgers**

Put the book down. Take your paper. Draw two columns.

On the left, write "Work Stress." List the stresses that would exist even if you shipped everything tomorrow: the deadlines, the difficulty, the genuine demands of your role.

On the right, write "Standard Stress." List the stresses created by the demand for flawlessness: the re-reading, the refusal to ship, the work carried into the weekend, the self-judgment.

Now look at the two lists. The right-hand list will be longer, and every item on it is optional. Cross it out. Every single item. The work remains. The siege ends.
