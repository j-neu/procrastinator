# Chapter 3: Why is it Difficult to Start?

All perfectionists believe that starting is the difficult part. "Once I get going, I'm fine. It's just getting going that kills me." So they wait for the perfect morning, the perfect headspace, the perfect hour when the work will flow.

They are wrong. Starting is not difficult. The difficult part is the mental gymnastics we perform *before* we start—the demand that the first draft be flawless.

Imagine you are asked to write a short note to a friend. You sit down. The words come. Done in two minutes. No problem.

Now imagine the same note will be published and judged. Suddenly the blank page feels like a wall. Why? The task didn't change. What changed is the *demand you placed on it*. You are no longer allowed to write a rough note. You must write a flawless note. And because a flawless note does not exist, you cannot start.

The difficulty was never starting. The difficulty is the rule you invented that says the first thing you produce must be excellent.

Perfectionists imagine that non-perfectionists find starting easy. In fact, they face the same blank page, the same doubt. The difference is they have only one rule: *write something, anything, and fix it later.* The perfectionist has a second rule bolted on top: *and it must be brilliant from the very first keystroke.*

That second rule is what makes starting feel like climbing a cliff. It is not a rule of nature. It is a rule you made up, and you can unpick it whenever you choose.

Here is a question to hold on to: **If you were the only person who would ever read the first draft, would you find it difficult to start?** No. You would just begin. The fear of starting is not a fear of the work. It is a fear of the judge. And the judge, in this case, is you.

There is another layer. Perfectionists believe that if they start too early, before the work is "ready," they will ruin it. They speak about their projects the way farmers speak about unripe fruit. But the fruit does not ripen in the drawer. It ripens on the tree. Work ripens in the doing. There has never been a perfect moment in the history of the world. There have been millions of good-enough moments, and the people who finish things grabbed one of those.

Think about the greatest work you have ever read. It was not flawless in the first draft. It was probably a mess. Someone made a mess, and then someone made it slightly less of a mess, again and again. The finished masterpiece was built from rough material, not born perfect. You can only polish something that exists. You cannot polish a blank page. Every hour spent waiting for the perfect start is an hour spent with the raw material still unformed. The non-perfectionist is not producing better first drafts than you. They are simply producing more of them, because they are not ashamed of the mess.

Here is the liberating truth: **starting is only difficult while you believe the starting line must be perfect.** The moment you give yourself permission to begin badly, beginning becomes effortless. It is the permission that is hard, not the beginning.

So let me give you that permission now. You have my permission to start badly. You have my permission to write a terrible first sentence. You have my permission to make a draft so rough that future-you will wince. Start anyway. The work does not care how you begin it. It only cares that you begin it.

**EXERCISE: The Two-Minute Rule**

Put the book down. Set a timer for two minutes. Choose the project you have been avoiding starting. Now write the worst possible version of the first step. Deliberately write something you would be embarrassed to show anyone.

When the timer goes off, read what you wrote. Notice that the world did not end. Notice that you have, for the first time in weeks, *started*.

That is all starting is. The rest is just editing.
