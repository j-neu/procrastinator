# Chapter 31: Avoid False Incentives

The perfectionist, in their quieter moments, knows that the polishing is a problem. And like everyone who knows they have a problem, they try to bribe themselves out of it.

"I'll treat myself to dinner if I ship this draft today."
"If I finish this report, I'll buy myself that thing I've been wanting."

These are false incentives, and they fail. They fail for reasons that are worth understanding, because the failure is not the bribery's fault. The failure is built into the perfectionist's psychology.

Here is the first problem. The perfectionist does not believe they deserve the reward until the work is perfect. The dinner, the purchase, the weekend off, all of them are contingent on the work being *good enough*. And "good enough" is a bar the perfectionist cannot define, because the bar is set by the fear. So the reward recedes. The draft ships, and the perfectionist immediately spots the flaw, and the flaw disqualifies the reward. "I can't treat myself to dinner. That draft was a mess." The incentive was never a reward for shipping. It was a reward for perfection, and perfection never arrives.

Here is the second problem, and it is the deeper one. The incentive frames the shipping as a burden that must be compensated. "If I do this unpleasant thing, I get a treat." Every bribe is a declaration: the work is unpleasant, the effort is a sacrifice, and the only reason to do it is the reward at the end.

This is the opposite of the mindset that makes shipping easy. The person who ships easily does not ship for the reward. They ship because the work is the point. When you bribe yourself, you are teaching yourself that the work is a punishment and the reward is the only relief. You are reinforcing the very trap you are trying to escape.

There is a third problem, and it is the most subtle. The incentive puts the perfectionist in the position of parent and child at once. The parent says: "Do the work, and you may have the treat." The child says: "But the work isn't perfect, so I don't deserve the treat." The negotiation is endless, because the child in you has learned that the parent in you is unappeasable. You cannot bribe yourself out of a relationship with yourself that is fundamentally adversarial.

The free worker does not need incentives, and not because they are saintly. The shipping is not a sacrifice to be compensated. It is a relief to be enjoyed. The release of the work is the reward. The closing of the loop is the treat.

Now, you might be thinking: "But I need *some* motivation. I've been stuck for months." This is the moment to be honest about what has been driving you. It was never the incentive. It was the fear. You have been moving, or failing to move, not because of the promise of reward but because of the threat of judgment. No dinner has ever been big enough to outbid the terror of the flawed release. That is why the incentives fail, not because they are weak, but because they are competing against the strongest force in your psychology, and they are losing.

The way out is not a bigger incentive. The way out is removing the competitor. When the fear of judgment is gone, the work becomes what it always was: a thing to be done. And the doing becomes its own reward. The perfectionist has been so busy bribing and dreading that they have never noticed the work itself is not unpleasant. The work is the most absorbing, satisfying thing a human being can do. The dread was the unpleasant part. Remove the dread, and the work reveals its nature.

So stop bribing. The incentives are a distraction. They are the perfectionist's version of negotiating with a fear they have not yet faced. The fear must be faced, not bargained with.

There is one exception, and I mention it only to prevent a misunderstanding. You may reward yourself for a milestone *after* the work is done, as a celebration, not a bribe. The difference is timing and intention. A bribe is offered before, as a condition. A celebration is given after, as an acknowledgment. The bribe is a deal with the fear. The celebration is a gift to yourself. One is a trap. The other is a pleasure.

Learn the difference, and you will find the celebrations become more frequent, because the shipping becomes more frequent, and the fear becomes quieter, and the work finally becomes what it was always meant to be: the thing you do because you are free.

**EXERCISE: The Bribe Inventory**

Put the book down. Take your paper. Write down every incentive you have ever offered yourself to ship. The dinners, the purchases, the promised days off.

Now write what happened to each one. Did you earn it? Or did the "not good enough" voice disqualify it?

You will notice the pattern: the bribe was never collected, because the work was never released, or was released and immediately judged unworthy.

Now write the sentence that ends the bribery: *"I do not need to bribe myself to be free. The release is the reward. The fear was the only thing that made it feel like a punishment."*

Then ship the next thing, without the bribe. You will discover that the shipping was never the price of the pleasure. It was the pleasure.
